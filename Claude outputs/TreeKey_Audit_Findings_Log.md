## Pricing / unit economics (thread closed)

- Hold £19 / £29 / £39 / £49 one-off prices steady. No further pricing redesign until real pilot evidence exists.
- Do not build the proposed 5-category reclassification (Starter/Growth/Consultant/Commercial & Forestry/Elite) or the two new price points (£35/£69) — shelved pending data.
- Run an individual-lead pilot *before* any subscription pilot — they test different questions and shouldn't be run together.
- Pilot design: (1) follow up with the existing £19 buyer first — ask what happened, would they buy again; (2) manually inspect 30 recent leads across price bands for "contractor already appointed" / "survey already done" / stale-listing signals, keep "unknown" separate from "confirmed available"; (3) recruit 5-10 contractors for a small paid pilot (target 20-30 purchases total) with accurately described leads, don't push unsuitable purchases; (4) track everything in one spreadsheet — price, buyer, application age, work type, approach made, response, quote, outcome, refund, repeat purchase; follow up at ~2 and ~6 weeks, keep long-running jobs open rather than marking them failed early; (5) diagnose the actual cause before changing any price again.
- Core metric: **lead fees per job won = total lead spend ÷ jobs actually won.** Not a hypothetical conversion-rate assumption.
- Don't apply a separate "competition adjustment" on top of a measured cost-per-job-won — that double-counts, since actual win/loss outcomes already reflect competitive loss.
- Judge lead affordability against the buyer's own job economics first (does the job leave enough margin after the lead fee and their own effort) — competitor benchmarking is secondary context, not the primary test.
- Subscription entitlements (what's included, claim limits, what happens when supply runs out) must be explicitly defined and fixed for a pilot period *before* selling more subscriptions. Enforce manually and track during the pilot rather than building quota code prematurely; log every manual exception separately so it doesn't contaminate the data.
- Always verify competitor pricing against the platform's own official page, not third-party aggregator blogs. Caught two real errors this way: Bark is £1.80/credit (official help centre), not £1.20 (aggregator blog); Checkatrade's own pricing page doesn't support the "£5-40/lead on top of membership" claim.
- The "Consultant" one-off category (BS5837 survey leads) needs verification before it's trusted as a sellable category: check whether a BS5837 report is typically already submitted *with* the planning application by the time it's scraped — if so, there may be no outstanding survey work left to sell.

## Legal / compliance (thread in progress)

- Free-lead signups and one-to-one phone calls are **not** automatically safe recruitment/marketing channels — check what consent was actually captured at signup; live calls still require TPS/CTPS screening regardless of how the number was obtained.
- A warmed sending domain solves deliverability, not legal permission to send marketing — separate problems, don't conflate them.
- **Biggest newly-identified risk, verified directly against ICO's own guidance:** TreeKey's core pipeline (scraping homeowner details from planning applications and selling them to contractors) likely triggers a UK GDPR Article 14 duty to proactively notify each homeowner, within about one month, that their information was collected and is being used/sold for marketing purposes — and a website privacy policy link does **not** satisfy this (ICO is explicit that passive disclosure is inadequate; must "proactively make individuals aware"). This is separate from, and arguably more fundamental than, the already-known PECR cold-email-to-contractors problem, because it concerns the core product itself, not just outreach to contractors.
- Narrow question for a solicitor, not a generic policy rewrite: *"Can we collect these exact planning fields and sell them once for contractor outreach, and precisely what must we tell the applicant, when and how?"*
- Recommended low-cost sequence: (1) now — exclude unconsented sole traders/uncertain businesses from any cold email, confirm credential rotation; (2) next — prepare a one-page data-journey map, one redacted example lead, the current privacy notice, and buyer terms; (3) first paid step — fixed-fee solicitor review of the planning-data resale model specifically, using the question above; (4) then — implement whatever notice/objection/retention process is required before scaling further, and check the ICO registration fee tier (currently £52/year for tier one, if applicable).
- A "genuinely neutral" privacy notice (no promotion, no encouragement to act) isn't classed as PECR direct marketing — confirmed against ICO's own page. But don't over-read that: it settles the marketing-classification question only, not delivery-channel rules or GDPR's own "effective means" requirement for the notice. And explaining how to exercise a privacy right isn't itself "promotional" — it doesn't need to be scrubbed out of a compliant notice.
- Real facts pulled directly from the live code (not description from memory): applicant name, agent name/company, full address, and full application text are all captured at scan time for every tree-relevant application (~2,700/month) — no phone/email is ever held for the homeowner (councils don't publish either; a `homeowner_contact` column exists but has never been populated by any code path). Non-tree-relevant applications (e.g. HMO/care-home) are scraped and stored with the same personal-data fields even though they're excluded from the marketplace — a population with no current commercial purpose at all.
- Retention, as it actually exists in code: unsold ('new'-status) leads — tree and non-tree alike — are auto-deleted after 56 days if both their registered date and discovery date are that old. Sold/claimed leads have no deletion policy at all and persist indefinitely. This is a business-relevance rule, not a designed data-protection retention policy, and nobody has evaluated whether either duration is actually justified.
- Objection handling: there is currently no mechanism by which a homeowner/applicant could object at all, let alone have it enforced against a future re-scan of the same address. The only suppression system in the codebase (`email_suppressions`) is scoped to contractor/subscriber marketing emails opting out of TreeKey's own outreach — a different population, keyed by an email address homeowners don't have on file in the first place. This is a real, currently-unfilled gap against the GDPR right to object.
- Public-facing redaction (address stripped from marketplace listings and summaries) is implemented as best-effort regex, explicitly not guaranteed complete in the code's own comments — worth spot-checking real outputs for leaks before treating it as a safeguard in any compliance argument.
- Confirmed directly in code: rediscovery does NOT reset the 56-day retention clock. `discovered_at` is set once on first INSERT and is never touched by the daily rescan's `ON CONFLICT ... DO UPDATE` (which only backfills previously-empty fields). The rule does what it appears to do.
- Remediation priority table agreed: (1) applicant objections — visible contact route, monitored process, proportionate ID checks, logged actions; (2) suppression — must survive even after the underlying record is deleted, and matching needs care (an application-reference-only block misses later applications at the same address; an address-only block could wrongly catch a future different occupant); (3) retention — separate, justified rules for unsold leads, sold leads, transaction/payment records, suppression records, and backups (a payment record needing longer retention doesn't justify keeping the full applicant record indefinitely); (4) unnecessary records — resolve whether non-tree personal data has any justified purpose, reduce collection/retention if not; (5) public listings — test real output, don't call best-effort redaction "anonymised"; (6) already-disclosed records — define what happens to data already sent to a buyer when an objection/correction/erasure request comes in later.
- "Never resold" as a promise needs an exclusion/suppression mechanism, not permanent retention of every original personal-data field — a minimal marker (e.g. "this reference is sold, do not relist") can enforce the promise without keeping the full record forever.

## Go-to-market sequencing (thread in progress)

- Resequence the launch gate — cold email should be last, not first, since it recruits people into an offer whose safety, usefulness, and delivery haven't been established yet. New order: (1) safe to operate — resolve the planning-data/privacy questions above, confirm credentials rotated; (2) honest, working purchase — confirm (not rebuild) that payment reliably delivers the correct lead, per the already-fixed webhook chain; (3) useful product — can a buyer actually act on what they bought, is the underlying work still genuinely available; (4) simple paid offer — keep current one-off prices for testing, define subscription entitlements before selling any, don't need all 5 packages finished to test demand; (5) small acquisition pilot — recruit a few suitable buyers through an appropriate (compliant) channel, measure purchases/outcomes/repeat-purchases, expand only after.
- Free-lead redemption and a fully-automated cold-email sequence are both real features, but neither is an essential gate to reaching the first few paying customers — a buyer can purchase directly, and initial recruitment can be manual/personal rather than automated.
- Highest-leverage single next action: follow up directly with the existing £19 buyer. Ask whether they approached the applicant, what happened, what (if anything) was missing or inaccurate, and whether they'd buy another at the same price and why. Four possible answers point to four different fixes: couldn't use the info → fix the record/delivery; work already had a contractor → fix selection/timing; hasn't approached anyone yet → understand the obstacle before touching price; got a worthwhile response → study what made that one work and try to repeat it. One buyer isn't proof of anything, but it's the cheapest real evidence available.
- Worth folding into that same follow-up: confirm whether the actual lead-delivery/receipt email reached them after last week's webhook fix — that's the one piece of the payment-fix chain that was never independently confirmed, separate from the payment itself succeeding.
- Keep the delivery/usefulness service check and any "would you buy again" purchase-intent question as two separate contacts, not one message — blending them muddies both signals. If the buyer says the delivery email never arrived, the fix is to check the actual order/delivery record and resend or hand the details over directly — not ask them to pay again. A successful payment and a "sent" email log entry don't themselves prove successful delivery.

Thread closed: send the factual delivery/usefulness follow-up now, fix any delivery gap found, record the outcome, and keep the compliance work moving in parallel — hold off on any expanded acquisition until that's resolved.

## Lead category validity (thread in progress)

- Consultant and commercial/forestry candidates need separate testing — a completed survey only proves the survey was done, it says nothing about whether later clearance or supervision work has been commissioned.
- Sampling rules for this and any future spot-check: select reproducibly (not hand-picked), report how many eligible records actually exist before sampling, never pad a short list with off-category records to hit a round number.
- Four-way classification per record: (1) work already completed, (2) further work explicitly required but appointment unknown, (3) evidence a specific contractor is already appointed for that work, (4) insufficient evidence.
- When getting outside (consultant) feedback on sample records, show rejected/ambiguous examples alongside the strongest ones — showing only the best examples produces enthusiastic feedback about a hand-picked subset, not a usable read on typical supply.
- Source-link persistence (so future checks don't need manual portal lookup) is a good idea but explicitly scoped as a separate, later improvement — don't let it block or delay this inspection. A column alone doesn't solve it: needs reliable population across every scanner path, and URLs may not stay stable over time. Council + reference stays the fallback lookup method regardless.
- No new classifier or pricing category is justified until this sample shows what a real, reliable signal for "outstanding work" actually looks like.

## Competitive positioning (thread closed)

- Drop "no ad-spend-funded" from the sales pitch entirely — it's a TreeKey cost-structure fact, not a customer benefit. Avoiding advertising doesn't remove the work of acquiring a customer, it shifts it from "pay to get someone to raise their hand" to "the contractor has to introduce themselves cold." Customers care about their own total cost and effort per worthwhile job, not how cheaply TreeKey sourced the record.
- Don't claim exclusivity is unique in the market. PlanWatch (confirmed via their own pricing page) already sells a £149/month territory-exclusivity model — "your patch, your trade, nobody else" — a different mechanism from TreeKey's per-record exclusivity, but a real competing claim. "Exclusive distribution through TreeKey" also isn't the same thing as "exclusive access to the opportunity" — nothing stops another provider, or the contractor themselves, from finding the same public application, or the homeowner already having quotes. Be precise about what the exclusivity promise actually covers.
- "Earlier" is trade- and stage-dependent, not absolute — an application can be early for site-clearance work and simultaneously late for the survey attached to it (ties directly to the BS5837 timing question in the lead-category-validity thread). The real advantage claim has to be "correct timing for this specific kind of work," not "we found it first."
- Exclusivity on a lead that's already been actioned or assigned has little value — accurate selection and an actionable next step likely matter more than the exclusivity badge itself.
- Honest positioning to use now: "Find relevant tree-work opportunities without searching council portals. See the proposed work, application date and source before buying. Every purchased opportunity is sold once by TreeKey and never resold" — plus explicit framing that these are planning-derived opportunities, not homeowner quote requests (don't let it read like Bark/Checkatrade/Lead Pronto's model). A stronger claim ("we filter out already-appointed work") is earned only once the lead-category-validity test actually shows that's true.
- Overall commercial read: competitor pricing puts real pressure on £19-49 for bare application information alone — but the fix is proving better selection/timing/detail/outcomes through the pilot, not inventing a new lower price list. The pilot's real question stays: does a contractor choose to buy again because it was worth the money and effort. Current evidence is too thin to say the market has rejected TreeKey — it shows the differentiation is less unique than assumed and still financially unproven, which is a "test it properly" conclusion, not a "the business doesn't work" one.
- Correction on my own earlier work: my competitor research had hedged that Lead Pronto's leads were "likely non-exclusive" — that was my own inference, not something their page actually states, and it shouldn't have been treated as an established fact in the briefing.
